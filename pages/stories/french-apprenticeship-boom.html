The French apprenticeship boom

How France went from 425,876 to over 1,000,000 apprentices in seven years, who they are, and why the count is now falling.

Scroll to read ↓

At the end of 2017, 425,876 people in France were training under an apprenticeship contract. The number had hardly moved in years: 432,995 at the end of 2013.

In September 2018 a new law, “for the freedom to choose one’s professional future”, changed how apprenticeship works. Any training organisation could now open an apprentice training centre, and centres were funded for each contract they signed. By the end of 2019 there were 486,789 apprentices, +14% in two years.

In July 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, the government added an exceptional hiring aid: up to €5,000 for an apprentice under 18 and €8,000 for an adult in the first year. Instead of collapsing, the count jumped to 661,409 at the end of 2020, +36% in a single year.

The growth went on. At its peak, at the end of 2024, France had 1,048,840 apprentices: 2.5 times the 2017 level.

Who are the new apprentices? Mostly students in higher education. Apprentices preparing a higher-education diploma (BTS, bachelor, master, engineering degree) went from 167,048 to 634,017; those preparing a secondary-level diploma (CAP, vocational baccalaureate…) from 258,828 to 380,468. In 2025, 62% of apprentices were in higher education.

At the end of 2025 the count fell for the first time in the series: 1,014,485, −3% on a year earlier. The hiring aid had been cut, most recently in February 2025 to €5,000 for firms with fewer than 250 employees and €2,000 for larger ones. The latest monthly figure, for 2026-07, is 911,425, −3% on a year earlier.

Nothing comparable happened in the other countries with long national series. Since 2017: Norway +23% (to 2025); Switzerland +1% (to 2025); England −6% (to 2024). France also counts higher-education apprentices, which most countries do not, so compare the direction, not the levels.

Sources and method

End-of-December stocks of apprenticeship contracts from DARES (private and public employers, all levels). DARES treats the most recent 48 months as provisional. The comparison uses each country's own series indexed to 2017 = 100.
