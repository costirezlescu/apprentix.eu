Two families of apprenticeship

Europe’s 34 apprenticeship schemes look alike from afar. Sorted by Cedefop’s own answers, they split into two families with different histories, contracts and qualifications.

Scroll to read ↓

Cedefop’s database describes 34 apprenticeship schemes in Europe, each coded on the same questions: where it came from, who the learner is, what contract they sign, what qualification they get. Each dot is one scheme.

17 schemes are old: introduced before 2000, 16 of them from a craft tradition, like Germany’s, Austria’s or Denmark’s dual systems.

Another 10 were introduced after 2012, and 14 schemes in all grew out of school-based vocational education: schools adding long periods of work to their programmes.

Sort the schemes by those answers and two families appear: 18 employer-based apprenticeships, where the learner has the status of an apprentice, and 14 school-based dual tracks. 2 fit neither: Professionalisation contract (France), Apprenticeship programmes (Portugal).

In the school-based family the learner often stays a student: in 9 of 14 schemes, against 0 of 18 employer-based ones.

A training agreement can replace the contract in 9 of 14 school-based schemes, against 1 of 18 employer-based ones.

And only employer-based schemes lead to a qualification you can get only through apprenticeship: 11 of them, against 0 school-based ones. In the others, the same qualification can also be earned in a school-based programme.

So which family works better? Neither group of countries clearly does better: median employment of young people who recently finished vocational training is 80.3% where schemes are employer-based and 78.8% where they are school-based, while vocational education graduates' advantage over general-education graduates is 7.9 and 11.4 points respectively.

Sources and method

Families from Cedefop's coded answers (2026 update): school-based dual = the scheme grew out of school-based vocational education; employer-based = the learner has a specific apprentice status and the scheme did not grow out of school-based vocational education. Outcomes compare countries whose schemes all fall in one family; they reflect all vocational education and training, not only apprentices.
