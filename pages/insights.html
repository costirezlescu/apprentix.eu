For decision-makers

EU 2025 targets: work-based learning met, employment of VET graduates missed

In 2025, 66.0% of recent VET graduates in the EU had work-based learning — above the 60% target (up from 60.3% in 2021). The employment rate of recent VET graduates was 80.1%, short of the 82% target. 13 of 24 member states with data meet the first target; only 8 of 26 meet the second.

  • Furthest from the work-based learning target: Romania (9.1%), Czechia (15.2%), Italy (22.0%), Cyprus (25.0%).
  • Furthest from the employment target: Greece (58.0%), Romania (63.4%), Italy (64.9%), Spain (68.8%).
  • Largest gains in graduate employment since 2021: Portugal (+11.9 points), Lithuania (+11.9 points), Italy (+11.0 points); largest falls: Sweden (-5.9 points), Netherlands (-4.4 points), Cyprus (-2.5 points).

For decision-makers

Where more VET graduates learn at work, more of them find jobs

Across 28 European countries, those where a larger share of recent VET graduates had work-based learning tend to have higher employment among those graduates (Spearman ρ = +0.41, a moderate association). The link holds after allowing for how strong each country's labour market is overall (partial ρ = +0.35).

  • Work-based learning is also associated with a larger employment advantage of VET graduates over general-education graduates (ρ = +0.34; +0.38 after the same adjustment; 27 countries).
  • Exceptions matter: Czechia and Norway achieve higher graduate employment than their level of work-based learning would suggest, while Spain and France do less well than theirs would suggest. Labour-market structure, the quality of placements and how programmes are mapped all play a part.

Vocational graduates find work more often — and the gender gap is smaller

In 2025, 80.1% of recent EU graduates with a vocational qualification were employed, against 66.8% of those with a general upper-secondary qualification — an advantage of 13.3 points. Since 2014 the advantage has stayed between 13 and 18 points.

  • Women with a recent vocational qualification are employed 3.3 points less often than men; among general-education graduates the gap is 9.7 points.
  • For learners choosing a route, this is the EU-wide picture: in some countries general education leads more often to further study, which this measure does not count as a success.

For decision-makers

Europe has two families of apprenticeship — and neither clearly wins

Grouping the 34 schemes by Cedefop's coded answers shows 18 employer-based apprenticeships (long traditions, a distinct apprentice status) and 14 school-based dual tracks that grew out of school VET, mostly introduced after 2012. Neither group of countries clearly does better: median employment of recent VET graduates is 80.3% where schemes are employer-based and 78.8% where they are school-based, while VET graduates' advantage over general-education graduates is 7.9 and 11.4 points respectively.

  • Employer-based schemes: Austria, Belgium — French community, Cyprus, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Romania.
  • School-based dual tracks: Belgium — Flanders, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Estonia, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden.
  • Young people neither in employment nor education: median 9.1% in employer-based countries vs 11.0% in school-based ones.
  • Design details within each family — pay, the share of time at work, the role of social partners — vary as much as between them; see the comparison matrix.

For decision-makers

National apprenticeship numbers are moving in very different directions

Since 2017, the number of apprentices in France has grown 2.4-fold, to 1,014,485 at the end of 2025. Over the same period: Norway +23% (to 2025); Switzerland +1% (to 2025); England -6% (to 2024).

  • France's growth coincides with its 2018 vocational training reform and the hiring aid introduced in 2020; its December 2025 figure is the first annual fall.
  • Each country counts something different (stocks, starts or entrants; with or without higher-education apprenticeships), so compare the direction of travel, not the levels.

In most countries apprenticeship is still a minority route within VET

Of the 31 schemes whose share is known, 23 enrol under 30% of the country's VET learners at that level; only 8 enrol more. EU-wide, the share of upper-secondary VET pupils in combined school- and work-based programmes rose from 22.1% in 2015 to 24.7% in 2024.

  • Schemes enrolling 30% or more of VET learners: Denmark (Apprenticeship (VET programmes)), France (Apprenticeship contract), Germany (Dual VET), Hungary (Dual vocational training), Iceland (Apprenticeship (certified trades)), Malta (MCAST apprenticeships), Norway (Upper secondary VET (2+2 model)), Poland (Vocational preparation of juvenile workers).

Employers pay apprentices almost everywhere; governments sweeten the deal

In 33 of 34 schemes employers pay the apprentice, and in 28 that pay is a taxable wage. Governments compensate with subsidies (25 schemes) and tax deductions (17). Only 2 schemes offer companies no financial incentive.

  • Pay is set by law in 25 schemes and by sectoral collective agreements in 14.
  • Levy-financed training funds existed in 7 of the 21 countries in Cedefop's financing database (Germany, Denmark, France, Hungary, Ireland, Netherlands, United Kingdom), tax incentives in 10 (2016–17).

For decision-makers

One in three national VET reforms concerns apprenticeship

Of 1,184 VET policy developments that Cedefop and ReferNet have tracked since 2015, 408 concern apprenticeship or work-based learning — 36% in 2015–20 and 35% in 2021–25. Only 5% were discontinued.

  • Beyond work-based learning itself, these reforms most often also address: Engaging VET stakeholders and strengthening partnerships in VET (169); Financial and non-financial incentives to learners, providers and companies (134); Ensuring equal opportunities and inclusiveness in education and training (131); Modernising VET standards, curricula, programmes and training courses (109).

Erasmus+ money for VET mobility has more than tripled — unevenly

EU-wide, Erasmus+ grants for VET mobility projects rose from €238 million (2021 call) to €833 million (2025), and projects from 2,925 to 6,890. Relative to VET pupils, funding is highest in Cyprus (€668 per pupil) and Lithuania, and lowest in Netherlands (€52) and Luxembourg.

  • Smaller and Baltic countries draw the most per VET pupil; large dual systems the least.
  • For learners: most VET schools and many training companies in Europe can now send apprentices abroad — check the accredited organisations directory.

The same master craftsperson certificate sits at different European levels

Master craftsperson qualifications are placed at EQF 6 in Austria, Germany; EQF 5 in Croatia, France, Luxembourg; EQF 4 in Montenegro, Slovenia.

  • The EQF level shown on a certificate and in Europass affects how it is recognised abroad and whether it opens doors to further study.
  • Initial apprenticeship qualifications cluster at EQF 3–4; see the qualification levels dataset for each country.

For learners: what to expect from an apprenticeship in Europe

In 28 of 34 schemes apprentices earn a wage; 24 lead to a qualification giving direct access to higher education; and in 8 many apprentices are over 24 — apprenticeship is not only for school-leavers.

  • Schemes where many apprentices are adults: Denmark (Apprenticeship (VET programmes)), Estonia (Workplace-based learning), Finland (Apprenticeship training), France (Professionalisation contract), Italy (Type 3: higher education apprenticeship), Luxembourg (Apprenticeship contract), Portugal (Apprenticeship programmes), Romania (Apprenticeship at the workplace).
  • Look up a country page for the scheme's duration, pay rules and the official fiche, and the Erasmus+ directory for organisations that send apprentices abroad.

Where employment is forecast to grow

Cedefop's short-term forecast for 2027 expects EU employment to grow fastest for professionals (+1.7%) and technicians and associate professionals (+0.5%), and to shrink for skilled agricultural and fishery workers (-1.6%) and craft and related trades workers (-0.8%).

  • Employment change is not the same as job openings: people retiring from shrinking occupations still need to be replaced, which this forecast does not show.
  • Many technician and associate-professional jobs are reachable through higher VET and apprenticeship at EQF 5–6.

For decision-makers

What Europe still can't measure about apprenticeships

There is no EU statistic counting apprentices. The closest proxy — pupils in combined school- and work-based VET — is reported as 'not applicable' for 8 member states (Cyprus, Czechia, Greece, Croatia, Italy, Lithuania, Portugal, Slovenia), and open, machine-readable national apprentice statistics exist for only 7 countries (Switzerland, Finland, France, Ireland, Netherlands, Norway, United Kingdom).

  • Completion rates, apprentice pay levels and employer participation are not published comparably anywhere.
  • A common EU definition and an annual count of apprenticeship contracts would let countries benchmark what matters most.
Method and caveats
