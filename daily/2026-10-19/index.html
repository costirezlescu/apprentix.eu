New publication · 15 October

Cedefop’s new RESET index scores skills in all 244 EU regions

Published on 15 October, the regional skills ecosystems index compares regions on skills supply, demand, environment and match. Central Macedonia, home to the Thessaloniki office, ranks 208th; Brussels-Capital ranks 101st.

Cedefop describes RESET as the first multidimensional EU regional index focused specifically on skills and vocational education and training. It covers 244 regions in all 27 Member States and draws on 37 indicators across four dimensions. Regions can compare their performance with national and EU averages and see how they rank against other regions.

The index was presented at “Europe’s skills revolution starts in its regions”, held on 8 October at the European Committee of the Regions in Brussels as part of the European Skills & VET Week. Cedefop’s newsletter links it to the European Skills High-Level Board’s call in July for stronger regional and sectoral skills ecosystems, to Regional Skills Partnerships under the Pact for Skills and to the Union of Skills.

Scores run from 1 to 100 relative to the other EU regions (version July 2026, reference year 2024). Stockholm is highest at 75.9 and Sud-Vest Oltenia lowest at 26.8. Central Macedonia scores 36.5, fourth of 13 Greek regions: its strongest dimension is skills match (55.3) and its weakest skills demand (23.6). Brussels-Capital scores 54.3, fifth of 11 Belgian regions.

Executive Director Jürgen Siebel said the first event “confirmed how strong the demand is for evidence that reflects territorial realities”.

At Cedefop

Country support · 15 October

Italy and Czechia join Cedefop project on skills for the age of AI

WORK.FUTURE, launched in Rome on 22 September, is a two-year project under the EU’s Technical Support Instrument, run with the Commission’s SG REFORM and both national authorities.

It follows a two-year cooperation with Estonia that began in December 2024. Cedefop will select up to five occupations, sectors or labour market segments in each country for in-depth analysis, alongside foresight workshops, stakeholder consultations and roundtables in two regions of each country. The findings will feed into action plans.

The newsletter gives three comparisons. In 2023, 26% of employment in both countries was in high-skilled jobs, against 38% across the EU. Adult learning participation was 29% in Italy and 21% in Czechia, against an EU average of 39%. In the JRC’s 2024 AIM-Work survey, 28% of workers in Italy and 34% in Czechia said they used AI tools at work.

New publication · 30 September

34 apprenticeship schemes show incremental quality gains, Cedefop finds

Published on 30 September, five years after Cedefop’s previous analysis of the European framework for quality and effective apprenticeships and ahead of the forthcoming European VET strategy.

The analysis covers schemes in the 27 Member States, Iceland and Norway. Criteria on learning and working conditions are usually addressed. On framework conditions the picture is mixed, with several indicators unevenly met.

Across criteria, the report finds an association between how far social partners or chambers take part in decision-making and coordination and how far they invest in implementation. Limited roles go with narrower or uneven coverage of quality criteria.

New publication · 30 September

Early leaving is a process, not an event, says Cedefop policy brief

The EU early leaving rate fell to 9.1% in 2025 against a 2030 target of below 8%. The brief sets out 20 intervention approaches.

The approaches cover system capacity, early identification, targeted support and re-engagement, drawing on Cedefop’s VET toolkit for tackling early leaving. Among the policy pointers: build early identification into VET systems, match support to learners’ circumstances and keep pathways back into learning open. Cedefop will launch the brief at a webinar with the Commission’s DG EAC on 2 December.

Statement · 16 October

Quality jobs best way out of poverty for those who can work, says Mînzatu

In a statement on 16 October, ahead of the International Day for the Eradication of Poverty, the Executive Vice-President said one in five Europeans is at risk of poverty and social exclusion.

She pointed to the first EU Anti-Poverty Strategy, adopted earlier this year, and to the European Pillar of Social Rights Action Plan target of reducing the number of people at risk by at least 15 million by 2030. The economy “cannot afford leaving almost 50 million people outside the labour market”, she said.

She noted the first-stage consultation of the European social partners, launched in July, on possible EU action for people facing significant barriers to the labour market, and an ESF+ call from May that supports Roma youth aged 15–29 who are not in employment, education or training.

Agencies and partners

Cedefop and EU-OSHA · 6 October

Safer jobs can help ease labour shortages, say Cedefop and EU-OSHA

A joint article sets EU-OSHA evidence on working conditions beside Cedefop’s intelligence on skills and labour shortages.

Shortage occupations it names include welders, electricians, plumbers, cooks, heavy truck drivers and health care assistants. Many share demanding or hazardous working environments, limited career prospects and, in some cases, insecure employment. In EU-OSHA’s 2025 OSH Pulse survey, 29% of workers reported stress, anxiety or depression caused or worsened by work, up from 27% in 2022.

“Training more people for occupations with poor retention prospects will not be enough if those jobs remain insecure, unhealthy or unrewarding,” the article says.

Meet the apprentices: Italy and Czechia

With Italy and Czechia starting work with Cedefop on skills for the age of AI, here is what an apprenticeship looks like in each, from Cedefop’s coded answers for the European database on apprenticeship schemes.

IT · Typical apprentice

Type 1: apprenticeship for vocational qualification (Italy)

CompanySchool

Around half

  • Pay Wage, paid by your employer
  • Arrangement Specific apprenticeship contract
  • Duration 6 months–3/4 yrs by qualification
  • Typical age 18–24
  • Leads to Upper secondary · access to higher education

What to expect on this scheme

You would most likely be between 18 and 24. You would sign an apprenticeship contract and have the legal status of an apprentice. You would spend about half your time at the company. You would earn a wage, set by law or by collective agreements for your sector, paid by your employer. It would take 6 months–3/4 years by qualification. You would finish with: Vocational qualification, professional or upper secondary diploma, NQF 3–4, which gives direct access to higher education.

CZ · Typical apprentice

Dual practical training (Czechia)

CompanySchool

Around half

  • Pay Allowance, paid by your employer
  • Arrangement Formal agreement, not a contract
  • Duration 3–4 yrs per framework programme
  • Leads to Upper secondary · access to higher education

What to expect on this scheme

You would stay a student, with a formal training agreement between you, your school and the company. You would spend about half your time at the company. You would receive an allowance rather than a wage, paid by your employer. It would take 3–4 years per framework programme. You would finish with: VET qualification (ISCED 344/353/354/655), which gives direct access to higher education.

Research

Debate · 13 October

Two VET scholars debate the capabilities approach in JVET’s 250th issue

Stephanie Allais argues that the approach offers VET research little; Leesa Wheelahan argues that it has some value when used within broader theories.

The debate was held on 22 May 2026 to mark the journal’s 250th issue. Allais says the approach’s emphasis on human flourishing is important but not new, that it is at its core based on individualism, and that its outcomes-focused stance “gives weak VET systems new ways to fail”. Wheelahan argues that it insists on ethical individualism while rejecting methodological individualism, and situates it among critical realism, Bernsteinian sociology and Marxism. In a short reply, Allais accepts that distinction but argues that “compatibility is not a contribution”.

New paper · 13 October

Double mismatch hits graduates hardest, Norwegian survey study finds

In the Norwegian Graduate Survey, 90% of those with horizontal mismatch were also overeducated.

Studying one form of mismatch without the other overstates its effect: the link between horizontal mismatch and job satisfaction falls by 15% once overeducation is controlled for, and the link between overeducation and satisfaction by 45%. For graduates facing both, the negative effect exceeds that of either alone.

Journal of Education and Work · Relevant to: Skills intelligence

New paper · 13 October

Swiss apprentices and trainers saw pandemic support differently

A mixed-methods study combined a survey of 1,380 vocational trainers with 16 focus groups involving 119 apprentices.

Trainers tended to report that workplace support was largely maintained. Apprentices described it as less spontaneous and less consistently accessible, and in firms with increased workload more often reported limited guidance, feedback and emotional support.

Vocations and Learning · Relevant to: Apprenticeships and work-based learning · Teachers and trainers

New paper · 15 October

Resilience buffers burnout among German VET teachers, study finds

A survey of 1,470 vocational teachers found task scope and complexity to be the most prevalent job demands.

Perceived demands were linked to lower well-being and job satisfaction and to more burnout. Of the resources studied, only resilience significantly moderated the effect of demands on burnout. The authors suggest reducing demands and building resilience during teacher training.

Insight · from Apprentix

EU 2025 targets: work-based learning met, employment of VET graduates missed

In 2025, 66.0% of recent VET graduates in the EU had work-based learning — above the 60% target (up from 60.3% in 2021). The employment rate of recent VET graduates was 80.1%, short of the 82% target. 13 of 24 member states with data meet the first target; only 8 of 26 meet the second.
