The one thing New paper · 13 October

Swiss apprentices and trainers saw pandemic support differently

A mixed-methods study combined a survey of 1,380 vocational trainers with 16 focus groups involving 119 apprentices.

Trainers tended to report that workplace support was largely maintained. Apprentices described it as less spontaneous and less consistently accessible, and in firms with more workload more often reported limited guidance, feedback and emotional support.

Cedefop Insight · 15 October

How VET and business work together: a new Cedefop Insight

Drawing on the Timeline of VET policies in the EU-27, Iceland and Norway, it finds cooperation moving beyond work placements.

VET-business cooperation now also covers skills foresight, governance, teacher training and entrepreneurship education. Examples include France’s Campus of Excellence Industry of the Future South and Spain’s Digitalízate, where 58% of certification completers are women. It says SMEs need brokers such as chambers and sector bodies.

New publication · 13 October

Policy pointers for Estonia’s skills forecasting system published

The report, dated 13 October, comes from a project run with Estonia’s Ministry of Education and Research and the Commission.

Based on desk research, consultations, peer learning and examples from other countries, it sets strategic directions for OSKA and Estonia’s wider skills governance, “intentionally indicative rather than prescriptive”, and may inspire other countries with similar reform plans.

Greece · 16 October

Greece lets 257 small apprenticeship-year classes run this year

The Ministry of Education has allowed 257 classes of the post-secondary apprenticeship year to run in 2026–2027 with fewer apprentices than its regulation sets.

Staffing is tight elsewhere: in the Cyclades, the local secondary teachers’ union says 130 teaching posts are still empty a month into term, and ESOS reports a new vocational upper-secondary school running with its director as its only teacher.

Germany · 12 October

Germany’s minimum apprentice pay rises to €764 in 2027

For apprenticeships starting in 2027, the legal minimum rises to €764 a month in the first year, up 5.6% on 2026.

Germany has had fewer apprentices than it needs for years, the paper notes, and the minimum, in law since 2020, is meant to help turn that round. Where a collective agreement exists, its rate applies. BIBB updates the amount each year.

Country in focus

France

France: a million apprentices, and the first fall in a decade

Apprentices under contract grew 2.4-fold from December 2017, to 1,014,485 at the end of 2025, but fell 3% over the year: the first annual fall since December 2015.

Much of the growth was in higher education: 62% of France’s apprentices now prepare a higher-education diploma, up from 39% at the end of 2017. France has two schemes in Cedefop’s database: the apprenticeship contract, where apprentices are most likely between 18 and 24, and the professionalisation contract, where many are over 24.

In numbers

  • France's “Apprenticeship tax”, a levy on companies that funds apprenticeship, dates back to 1925.
  • Only 3 schemes were built from scratch: France's Professionalisation contract, Greece's Post-secondary apprenticeship year and Italy's Type 3: higher education apprenticeship.
  • In 8 of 33 European apprenticeship schemes many apprentices are over 24 — apprenticeship is not only for school-leavers.

Being an apprentice in France

Apprenticeship contract (France)

CompanySchool

Mostly at work (≥2/3)

  • Pay Wage, paid by your employer
  • Arrangement Specific apprenticeship contract
  • Duration 6 months–3 yrs
  • Typical age 18–24
  • Leads to Upper secondary to doctorate (EQF 4–8) · access to higher education

What to expect on this scheme

You would most likely be between 18 and 24. You would sign an apprenticeship contract and have the legal status of an apprentice. You would spend most of your time (two-thirds or more) at the company. You would earn a wage, set by law or by collective agreements for your sector, paid by your employer; it also covers your days at school. It would take 6 months–3 years. You would finish with: RNCP-registered qualifications, EQF 4–8, which gives direct access to higher education.

What to expect on this scheme

You would most likely be over 24 — many apprentices here are adults. Your training would be set out in a written arrangement (specific apprenticeship contract). You would spend most of your time (two-thirds or more) at the company. You would earn a wage, set by law, paid by your employer; it also covers your days at school. Duration: normally 6–12 months (most exceed 9). You would finish with: RNCP or branch-recognised qualifications (upper secondary to master), which gives direct access to higher education.
