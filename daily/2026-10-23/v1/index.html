Germany’s first carpentry world champion, 1967, still in the workshop at 78

He won gold at the forerunner of WorldSkills in Madrid at 18, ran the family firm for decades and trained most of its staff himself.

Albert Baumann grew up in a carpentry firm in Flein that his grandfather founded in 1896; as a child, he says, he was already working with a fretsaw. He began his apprenticeship in the family firm in 1964 and finished first at every level of the trade competitions, from guild to chamber to state. In 1966 he flew for the first time, to the national final in Berlin, his big carpenter’s saw stowed behind the back row on the airport staff’s advice. He won there too, at 18, and was honoured by the Federal President.

In July 1967 the German team went by coach from Duisburg to Madrid for the 16th International Vocational Training Competition, the forerunner of WorldSkills. The task was a hipped roof for a building with an extension, in three days. He finished first, after two, and used the spare time for sightseeing; he still talks about the Prado. On 17 July he took gold, ahead of young carpenters from Korea, the Netherlands, Ireland, Japan, Great Britain and Switzerland. Back in Flein, he recalls, customers came by to congratulate him, and some joked that prices would now go up, with a world champion in the workshop.

He qualified as a master carpenter in 1970 and took over the business from his father three years later. At its peak it employed twelve people, most of whom he had trained himself, and for decades he volunteered for the trade’s young people in the local carpenters’ guild. In 2012 he handed the firm to his children, the fourth generation. At 78 he still works in the workshop. Since 1967, four more German carpenters have won world gold, in 1973, 2003, 2015 and 2019.

The one thing New paper · 9 October

Teaching in Swedish prisons is a practice of its own, study finds

Interviews with 14 adult educators in Swedish prisons show a pedagogy built on trust, responsibility and empowerment.

Prison education is not simply adult education delivered under constraints, the authors argue, but a practice of its own, shaped by institutional rules, security routines and students’ varied educational backgrounds, and aimed at the students’ holistic development. It is negotiated between care and control, autonomy and coercion, trust and vigilance.

From the archive

Published 22 October 2008 · 18 years ago

When 764 VET experts went to learn from each other abroad

Cedefop’s review of the 2007 study visits programme counted 59 visits on 11 themes, for VET specialists, policy-makers and social partners.

Themes included making vocational training more attractive for young people and adult learners, and developing entrepreneurship. Questionnaires from 2005 and 2006 were analysed for what the visits changed in participants’ organisations and work.

Since then
Mobility is now measured for learners too: in 2023, 5.3% of EU vocational learners spent at least 10 days abroad (today’s chart). Germany has the most Erasmus-accredited VET organisations, 692, ahead of France with 516.
